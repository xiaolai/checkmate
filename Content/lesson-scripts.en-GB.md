# Checkmate lesson scripts — British English

Published native course text with Cedar narration.

Lessons play continuously; standalone puzzles are separate.

## 1. Introduction

### Takeaways

- Two players face each other across the board: White has the light pieces and Black the dark ones.
- White always makes the first move, and then the players take turns.
- Each player starts with sixteen pieces on a board of sixty-four squares.
- The goal is checkmate: attacking the enemy king so that it has no escape.
- The king is never captured; the game ends as soon as it is checkmated.

### Narration

**intro:0** — Welcome to chess

Welcome to chess, a game for two players. One player has the light pieces and is called White; the other has the dark pieces and is called Black.

**intro:1** — Sixteen pieces each

Each player starts with sixteen pieces. White's are lined up on the two rows nearest to White: eight pawns in front and eight larger pieces behind.

**intro:2** — Black's army

Black's sixteen pieces stand the same way on the far side, facing White's army across the empty middle of the board.

**intro:3** — White moves first

White always makes the first move of the game. Here White pushes the pawn in front of the queen two squares forward.

**intro:4** — Taking turns

Then Black answers, pushing the pawn in front of Black's queen two squares forward. After that the turns alternate: White, Black, White, Black, one move at a time.

**intro:5** — No passing

On your turn you must make exactly one move. You cannot skip a turn, and you cannot move twice in a row.

**intro:6** — The board

The board has sixty-four squares in eight rows of eight, coloured light and dark. The Chessboard lesson shows how every square gets its name.

**intro:7** — Six kinds of piece

Each army has one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights and eight pawns. Every kind of piece moves in its own way.

**intro:8** — The king

The most important piece is the king. Each king starts in the middle of its back row, right beside its queen, facing the other king.

**intro:9** — Capturing

Usually, a piece captures by moving onto a square held by an enemy piece, which is then taken off the board. Here White's knight jumps onto a black pawn and captures it.

**intro:10** — The real goal

Capturing pieces helps, but the real goal is the enemy king. Here White's rook attacks the black king along a straight, open line. An attacked king is said to be in check.

**intro:11** — Escaping check

A king in check must escape at once. This king can step to either side, off the rook's line, so the game goes on.

**intro:12** — Checkmate

Now White's rook slides all the way to the far corner and checks the black king. The king's own pawns block its way forward, and the rook covers the rest of that back row, so there is no escape.

**intro:13** — Checkmate wins

A king in check with no way out is checkmated. Checkmate ends the game at once, and the player who gives it wins.

**intro:14** — The king is never taken

Notice that the king is never actually captured. The game is over the moment it is checkmated, so it never leaves the board.

**intro:15** — Other endings

Not every game ends in checkmate. A player may resign, which means giving up, and some games end in a draw, where nobody wins.

**intro:16** — Where chess came from

Chess grew from a game played in India about fifteen hundred years ago. It spread through Persia to Europe, where the rules we use today settled about five hundred years ago.

**intro:17** — What comes next

Next, The Chessboard lesson teaches the name of every square, and then you will meet the pieces one by one.

## 2. The Chessboard

### Takeaways

- The board has sixty-four squares, alternating light and dark.
- Each player has a light square in the right-hand corner nearest to them.
- Files are the columns, lettered a to h; ranks are the rows, numbered 1 to 8.
- To name a square, say its file letter first and then its rank number: e4, d6, h1.
- Diagonals are slanting lines of squares that all share one colour.

The rows that run across the board are called ranks. There are eight, numbered 1 to 8, starting from White's side.

**board:7** — The fourth rank

This highlighted row is the fourth rank. It is the fourth row up from White's side.

## 3. The Pawn

### Takeaways

- Each player starts with eight pawns, on the second rank for White and the seventh rank for Black.
- A pawn moves straight forward one square, and never backwards or sideways.
- A pawn that has not moved yet may advance two squares, as long as both are empty.
- A pawn captures one square diagonally forward, never straight ahead.
- A pawn with a piece directly in front of it is blocked and cannot move forward.
- A pawn is worth one point.

**pawn:0** — Meet the pawn

Meet the pawn, the smallest member of each army. Every player starts the game with eight of them.

**pawn:1** — Where the pawns start

White's eight pawns stand on the second rank, from a2 to h2. Black's eight pawns stand on the seventh rank, from a7 to h7.

Recording text: White's eight pawns stand on the second rank, from A two to H two. Black's eight pawns stand on the seventh rank, from A seven to H seven.

**pawn:2** — One step forward

Pawns walk straight ahead, usually one square per move; there is one exception you will see soon. This white pawn steps from g3 to g4.

Recording text: Pawns walk straight ahead, usually one square per move; there is one exception you will see soon. This white pawn steps from G three to G four.

**pawn:3** — Never backwards

A pawn can never move backwards or sideways. From g4 its only step is forward, to g5.

Recording text: A pawn can never move backwards or sideways. From G four its only step is forward, to G five.

**pawn:4** — Black's pawns face the other way

For Black, forward means towards the first rank, on White's side. This black pawn steps from b7 to b6.

Recording text: For Black, forward means towards the first rank, on White's side. This black pawn steps from B seven to B six.

**pawn:5** — Two squares on the first move

Recording text: On its very first move, a pawn may go two squares instead of one. This pawn on D two may step to D three or go straight to D four.

Recording text: The double step is a choice, not a rule. This pawn moves just one square, from F two to F three.

**pawn:8** — Only once

Now that this pawn has moved, its chance for a double step is gone. From f3 it can only go to f4.

Recording text: Now that this pawn has moved, its chance for a double step is gone. From F three it can only go to F four.

**pawn:9** — A blocked pawn

The pawn takes the knight by moving from g3 to h4. The knight leaves the board, and the pawn stands in its place.

Recording text: The pawn takes the knight by moving from G three to H four. The knight leaves the board, and the pawn stands in its place.

**pawn:13** — Two attacked squares

A pawn attacks the squares just ahead of it on each diagonal. This pawn on f4 attacks e5 and g5.

Recording text: A pawn attacks the squares just ahead of it on each diagonal. This pawn on F four attacks E five and G five.

**pawn:14** — No capturing straight ahead

A pawn cannot capture straight ahead. These two pawns on c4 and c5 block each other, and neither of them can move.

Recording text: A pawn cannot capture straight ahead. These two pawns on C four and C five block each other, and neither of them can move.

Recording text: A blocked pawn can still capture. When a black knight stands on B five, the white pawn takes it by moving from C four to B five.

Recording text: Pawns never capture backwards. The knights on C four and E four are behind the pawn on D five, so it cannot take them.

Black's pawns capture diagonally forward too, which for Black means towards the first rank. This black pawn takes the knight, moving from f5 to g4.

Recording text: Black's pawns capture diagonally forward too, which for Black means towards the first rank. This black pawn takes the knight, moving from F five to G four.

**pawn:18** — Worth one point

A pawn is worth one point, the smallest value of any piece. Yet pawns standing side by side, or guarding each other, are hard to get past.

## 4. Pawn Duel

### Takeaways

- Pawn Duel uses six pawns each, from b2 to g2 for White and from b7 to g7 for Black.
- White moves first, and the pawns move and capture just as in a real game.
- You win by reaching the far rank, by capturing every opposing pawn, or when your opponent has no move.
- In Pawn Duel pawns never promote, and en passant is not played.
- Before you push a pawn, check whether an opposing pawn could capture it.

### Narration

**pawn-duel:0** — A game of pawns

Pawn Duel is a game played with pawns alone. It is a fun way to practise moving and capturing with pawns.

**pawn-duel:1** — The starting position

Each side has six pawns. White's stand from b2 to g2, and Black's stand from b7 to g7.

Recording text: Each side has six pawns. White's stand from B two to G two, and Black's stand from B seven to G seven.

**pawn-duel:2** — White moves first

White moves first, and the players take turns. Pawns move as usual, so White's pawn makes a double step from e2 to e4.

Recording text: White moves first, and the players take turns. Pawns move as usual, so White's pawn makes a double step from E two to E four.

**pawn-duel:3** — Black replies

Black answers with a double step too, from d7 to d5. Now the two pawns attack each other.

Recording text: Black answers with a double step too, from D seven to D five. Now the two pawns attack each other.

**pawn-duel:4** — Capturing as usual

Pawns capture diagonally, just as in a real game. White's pawn takes on d5, moving from e4 to d5.

Recording text: Pawns capture diagonally, just as in a real game. White's pawn takes on D five, moving from E four to D five.

**pawn-duel:5** — Win by reaching the end

The first way to win is to reach the far rank. This white pawn on c7 is one step from c8. If it gets there, White wins at once.

Recording text: The first way to win is to reach the far rank. This white pawn on C seven is one step from C eight. If it gets there, White wins at once.

**pawn-duel:6** — Black reaches rank 1

Black aims the other way. A black pawn that reaches the first rank wins the game, so this pawn on f2 needs just one more step.

Recording text: Black aims the other way. A black pawn that reaches the first rank wins the game, so this pawn on F two needs just one more step.

**pawn-duel:7** — Win by capturing them all

The second way is to capture every opposing pawn. White takes Black's last pawn, moving from d5 to e6. Every black pawn is now gone, and the game goes to White.

Recording text: The second way is to capture every opposing pawn. White takes Black's last pawn, moving from D five to E six. Every black pawn is now gone, and the game goes to White.

**pawn-duel:8** — Win when they cannot move

The third way: your opponent has no move on their turn. White's pawn steps from b4 to b5.

Recording text: The third way: your opponent has no move on their turn. White's pawn steps from B four to B five.

**pawn-duel:9** — Black is stuck

Now both black pawns are blocked, and nothing stands diagonally in front of them to capture. Black has no move, so White wins.

**pawn-duel:10** — No promotion, no en passant

In Pawn Duel a pawn never turns into another piece, and one special pawn capture is left out. You will meet both in Promotion and En Passant.

**pawn-duel:11** — Look before you push

Look before you push. If this white pawn moves to e5, the black pawn on d6 can capture it.

Recording text: Look before you push. If this white pawn moves to E five, the black pawn on D six can capture it.

**pawn-duel:12** — Guard your pawns

A pawn guarded by another pawn is hard to win. If Black's pawn on e5 takes on d4, the pawn on c3 takes it back.

Recording text: A pawn guarded by another pawn is hard to win. If Black's pawn on E five takes on D four, the pawn on C three takes it back.

**pawn-duel:13** — Count the race

When two pawns race, count the steps. White's pawn on g4 needs four moves to reach g8, and Black's pawn on b6 needs five to reach b1.

Recording text: When two pawns race, count the steps. White's pawn on G four needs four moves to reach G eight, and Black's pawn on B six needs five to reach B one.

## 5. The Rook

### Takeaways

- The rook moves in straight lines, along ranks and files, never diagonally.
- It can travel any distance in one move, but only while the path is clear.
- The rook cannot jump over pieces, its own or the enemy's.
- It captures by landing on an enemy piece's square, which removes that piece.
- A rook is worth five points.

### Narration

**rook:0** — Meet the rook

This tall piece with a notched top, like a castle tower, is the rook. Each player begins with two rooks in the corners, with the king between them: White's on a1 and h1, Black's on a8 and h8.

Recording text: This tall piece with a notched top, like a castle tower, is the rook. Each player begins with two rooks in the corners, with the king between them: White's on A one and H one, Black's on A eight and H eight.

**rook:1** — Straight lines

The rook moves in straight lines. It can go up or down its file, or sideways along its rank.

**rook:2** — Any distance

It may travel as far as it likes in one move. Here the rook slides from c5 all the way along the rank to h5.

Recording text: It may travel as far as it likes in one move. Here the rook slides from C five all the way along the rank to H five.

**rook:3** — Counting its squares

Back on c5, let's count every square the rook could reach in one move. There are fourteen of them.

Recording text: Back on C five, let's count every square the rook could reach in one move. There are fourteen of them.

**rook:4** — Fourteen from anywhere

On an empty board, the rook reaches fourteen squares from any square at all, even from a corner like h8: seven down the file and seven along the rank.

Recording text: On an empty board, the rook reaches fourteen squares from any square at all, even from a corner like H eight: seven down the file and seven along the rank.

**rook:5** — Never diagonally

The rook never moves diagonally. The four squares touching c5 at the corners are out of its reach in one move.

Recording text: The rook never moves diagonally. The four squares touching C five at the corners are out of its reach in one move.

**rook:6** — A clear path

The rook cannot jump over anything. White's own pawn on d6 blocks the file, so d7 and d8 are out of reach.

Recording text: The rook cannot jump over anything. White's own pawn on D six blocks the file, so D seven and D eight are out of reach.

**rook:7** — Blocked on the rank

The pawn on f4 does the same along the rank. The rook can stop on e4, but g4 and h4 are behind the pawn.

Recording text: The pawn on F four does the same along the rank. The rook can stop on E four, but G four and H four are behind the pawn.

**rook:8** — Not on its own pieces

A rook can never land on a square held by its own side. To this rook, the white pawns are walls, not targets.

**rook:9** — Capturing

The rook captures the same way it moves. Here it can reach Black's knight on d7 and Black's pawn on g4.

Recording text: The rook captures the same way it moves. Here it can reach Black's knight on D seven and Black's pawn on G four.

**rook:10** — Taking the knight

The rook moves from d4 to d7 and takes the knight's place. The captured knight leaves the board for good.

Recording text: The rook moves from D four to D seven and takes the knight's place. The captured knight leaves the board for good.

**rook:11** — Only the first piece

A rook can capture only the first enemy piece in its line. The rook on a1 can take the pawn on a5, but the queen on a8, behind that pawn, is out of reach.

Recording text: A rook can capture only the first enemy piece in its line. The rook on A one can take the pawn on A five, but the queen on A eight, behind that pawn, is out of reach.

**rook:12** — Open lines

Rooks love open lines. The rook on d1 sees all the way up the empty d-file, while the rook on a1 is blocked by its own pawn on a2 and can only shuffle along the first rank.

Recording text: Rooks love open lines. The rook on D one sees all the way up the empty d-file, while the rook on A one is blocked by its own pawn on A two and can only shuffle along the first rank.

**rook:13** — Rooks protect each other

Two rooks on the same rank or file, with nothing between them, guard each other. If one is captured, the other can take back.

**rook:14** — Worth five points

A rook is worth five points, as much as five pawns. Of the pieces that are counted in points, only the queen is worth more.

**rook:15** — Look before you capture

So check before you capture. The rook could take the pawn on d5, but Black's pawn on e6 would then capture the rook: five points lost for one.

Recording text: So check before you capture. The rook could take the pawn on D five, but Black's pawn on E six would then capture the rook: five points lost for one.

**rook:16** — Summary

The rook moves in straight lines, any distance, never through pieces. It captures by landing on an enemy piece, and it is worth five points.

## 6. Rook Practice

### Takeaways

- In Rook Sweep only the rooks move, and you win by capturing every opposing pawn.
- In Rook Sweep, losing your rook, or having no move, loses the game.
- In Rooks and Pawns everything moves normally, and a pawn on the far rank stays a pawn.
- In Rooks and Pawns you win by capturing every opposing piece, or by reaching the far rank with a pawn that survives the next move.
- Before every move, check whether the enemy rook can capture the piece you move.

### Narration

**rook-practice:0** — Two rook games

Let's put the rook to work. Here are two games: Rook Sweep and Rooks and Pawns.

**rook-practice:1** — Rook Sweep

This is Rook Sweep. Each side has one rook and seven pawns. White's rook starts on b1, and Black's rook starts on g8.

Recording text: This is Rook Sweep. Each side has one rook and seven pawns. White's rook starts on B one, and Black's rook starts on G eight.

**rook-practice:2** — Only the rooks move

Only the rooks move. The pawns never move at all: they are targets. White moves first.

**rook-practice:3** — Sweep up the pawns

Capture every opposing pawn to win. White's rook can start at once: it slides from b1 to b6 and takes Black's pawn.

Recording text: Capture every opposing pawn to win. White's rook can start at once: it slides from B one to B six and takes Black's pawn.

**rook-practice:4** — Black replies

Black's rook answers by sliding from g8 down to g3 and capturing White's pawn there.

Recording text: Black's rook answers by sliding from G eight down to G three and capturing White's pawn there.

**rook-practice:5** — Guard your rook

The rooks can capture each other too, and a side that loses its rook loses the game. A side with no move on its turn also loses.

**rook-practice:6** — A tempting pawn

Imagine this moment later in a game. White's rook on a7 can capture the pawn on d7. Is that a good idea?

Recording text: Imagine this moment later in a game. White's rook on A seven can capture the pawn on D seven. Is that a good idea?

**rook-practice:7** — The trap

White's rook captures the pawn on d7. Black's rook then captures White's rook from h7, along the empty seventh rank, and Black wins.

Recording text: White's rook captures the pawn on D seven. Black's rook then captures White's rook from H seven, along the empty seventh rank, and Black wins.

**rook-practice:8** — Check the lines

So before every move, look along the rank and the file of the square you are going to. If the enemy rook can reach it, choose another move.

**rook-practice:9** — Rooks and Pawns

The second game is Rooks and Pawns. Each side has two rooks and six pawns. White's rooks start on d1 and e1, and Black's on d8 and e8.

Recording text: The second game is Rooks and Pawns. Each side has two rooks and six pawns. White's rooks start on D one and E one, and Black's on D eight and E eight.

**rook-practice:10** — Everything moves

Here pawns move too. They step forward and capture diagonally onto an enemy piece, and rooks slide along open lines.

**rook-practice:11** — Face to face

The rooks face each other from the very first move. White's rook captures from d1 to d8. Black's rook on e8 captures it straight back.

Recording text: The rooks face each other from the very first move. White's rook captures from D one to D eight. Black's rook on E eight captures it straight back.

**rook-practice:12** — Winning by capture

One way to win is to capture every opposing piece, rooks and pawns alike. A side with no move on its turn loses.

**rook-practice:13** — Winning with a pawn

The other way is to reach the far rank with a pawn, which simply stays a pawn there. White's pawn on c7 is one step from c8, but Black's rook on h8 could capture it there.

Recording text: The other way is to reach the far rank with a pawn, which simply stays a pawn there. White's pawn on C seven is one step from C eight, but Black's rook on H eight could capture it there.

**rook-practice:14** — A pawn that survives

A pawn on the far rank wins only if it survives the opponent's next move. With Black's rook on h5 instead, nothing could reach c8, so stepping there would win.

Recording text: A pawn on the far rank wins only if it survives the opponent's next move. With Black's rook on H five instead, nothing could reach C eight, so stepping there would win.

**rook-practice:15** — Draws

In both games, the same position three times is a draw, and so are forty turns without a capture or a pawn move.

**rook-practice:16** — Your turn

## 7. The Bishop

### Takeaways

- The bishop moves along diagonals, any distance, while the path is clear.
- Whatever colour of square a bishop begins on, it keeps for the whole game.
- Each player has one light-squared bishop and one dark-squared bishop.
- The bishop captures by landing on an enemy piece and cannot jump over pieces.
- A bishop is worth three points.

### Narration

**bishop:0** — Meet the bishop

Meet the bishop, the piece with a pointed, slanted top. Each player starts with two, either side of the queen and king: White's on c1 and f1, Black's on c8 and f8.

Recording text: Meet the bishop, the piece with a pointed, slanted top. Each player starts with two, either side of the queen and king: White's on C one and F one, Black's on C eight and F eight.

## 14. Safe Squares

### Takeaways

- A square is attacked when an enemy piece could reach it by its normal movement.
- Pawns attack the two squares diagonally in front of them, not the square straight ahead.

- The king cannot capture an enemy piece that another enemy piece protects.
- Before a king walk, mark every attacked square and plan a route over the safe ones.

## 15. Check and Checkmate

### Takeaways

- Check means the king is attacked, and it must be answered on the very next move.
- Check can be answered in three ways: step the king to safety, put a piece in the way, or take the attacker.

- Checkmate is a check with no way out, and it ends the game at once.

## 17. Castling

### Takeaways

- In castling the king slides two squares towards a rook, which then hops over and lands on the king's far side.

## 18. Castling Rules

### Takeaways

- Nothing may stand between the king and the rook you castle with.
- You can never castle if the king has moved, or with a rook that has moved.
- You cannot castle out of check, through an attacked square, or into check.
- In queenside castling the rook may pass over an attacked b1 or b8; only the king's squares must be safe.
- A blocked path or an attack can change later, but a king or rook that has moved has lost its castling for good.

### Narration

## 20. Draws

### Takeaways

- A draw means neither player wins; each usually scores half a point.
- Stalemate: the player to move is not in check and has no legal move. The game is drawn at once.
- When neither side can possibly give checkmate, such as king against king, the game is drawn.
- The same position three times, or fifty moves each without a capture or pawn move, lets the player to move claim a draw.
- Five times the same position, or seventy-five moves each without a capture or pawn move, ends the game as a draw automatically.
- Players may also agree to a draw.

### Narration

**draws:0** — Not every game is won

Not every chess game has a winner. When a game is drawn, nobody wins, and in a tournament each player usually scores half a point.

**draws:1** — Five kinds of draw

A game can be drawn in five main ways: stalemate, not enough pieces to checkmate, repeating a position, a long run of moves with no capture or pawn move, and agreement.

**draws:2** — A winning position

Here Black has only a king left, while White has a king and a queen. White should win, but must be careful.

**draws:3** — White moves the queen

White's queen moves from c5 to c7. Now it is Black's turn.

Recording text: White's queen moves from C five to C seven. Now it is Black's turn.

**draws:4** — No legal move

Black's king on a8 is not in check. But the queen attacks a7, b7 and b8, so the king has nowhere to go, and Black has no other piece to move.

Recording text: Black's king on A eight is not in check. But the queen attacks A seven, B seven and B eight, so the king has nowhere to go, and Black has no other piece to move.

**draws:5** — Stalemate

This is stalemate. When the player to move is not in check but has no legal move, the game ends at once as a draw.

**draws:6** — Check or no check

How is this different from checkmate? A checkmated king is attacked and has no escape. Here the king is not attacked at all; Black simply has no move to play.

**draws:7** — Blocked pawns count too

Stalemate can happen with other pieces still on the board. If it is Black's turn here, the pawn on a5 is blocked by White's pawn on a4, and every square around the king is attacked.

Recording text: Stalemate can happen with other pieces still on the board. If it is Black's turn here, the pawn on A five is blocked by White's pawn on A four, and every square around the king is attacked.

**draws:8** — Another stalemate

Black's king on h8 is not in check, and Black has no legal move, so this is stalemate too. When you are winning, make sure each move leaves your opponent a legal move, unless your move is checkmate.

Recording text: Black's king on H eight is not in check, and Black has no legal move, so this is stalemate too. When you are winning, make sure each move leaves your opponent a legal move, unless your move is checkmate.

**draws:9** — Not enough material

A game is also drawn when neither side can possibly give checkmate. With only two kings left, checkmate can never happen, so the game is over.

**draws:10** — A lone bishop or knight

A king and one bishop cannot checkmate a lone king, and neither can a king and one knight. These games are drawn as well.

**draws:11** — A pawn is different

A single pawn is a different story. It might reach the last rank and become a queen, so the game goes on.

**draws:12** — Repeating a position

Now a draw by repetition. Black has two rooks and a knight against White's queen, so Black is ahead. White's best hope is to keep giving check.

**draws:13** — The position appears once

White's queen moves from d7 to e8, giving check. Black's king steps from g8 to h7. Remember this position.

Recording text: White's queen moves from D seven to E eight, giving check. Black's king steps from G eight to H seven. Remember this position.

**draws:14** — The position appears twice

White's queen checks from e4. Black's king goes back to g8. White's queen checks from e8 again. Black's king returns to h7. The same position has appeared a second time.

Recording text: White's queen checks from E four. Black's king goes back to G eight. White's queen checks from E eight again. Black's king returns to H seven. The same position has appeared a second time.

**draws:15** — The position appears three times

White's queen checks from e4. Black's king goes to g8. White's queen checks from e8. Black's king goes to h7. This is the third time.

Recording text: White's queen checks from E four. Black's king goes to G eight. White's queen checks from E eight. Black's king goes to H seven. This is the third time.

**draws:16** — Claiming the draw

A position counts as repeated only if the same side is to move and the same moves are available, castling included. After three such repeats, the player to move can claim a draw, even if they were not in a row.

**draws:17** — Five times ends it

If nobody claims, play goes on. But if the same position appears five times, the game is drawn automatically.

**draws:18** — The fifty-move rule

Another rule stops endless games. If each player makes fifty moves with no capture and no pawn move, the player whose turn it is may claim a draw.

**draws:19** — Seventy-five moves

After seventy-five moves each with no capture and no pawn move, the game is drawn automatically, unless the last move gave checkmate.

**draws:20** — Agreeing a draw

Finally, once both have made a move, the players can agree to a draw, though some events limit this. Offer it just after making your move; your opponent may accept, or decline by playing on.

**draws:21** — Draws in summary

So a game can end in a draw by stalemate, not enough material, repetition, the fifty-move rule or agreement. A draw is half a point: much better than a loss.

## 24. Good Manners at the Board

### Takeaways

- Greet your opponent before the game and shake hands after it.
- Stay quiet during play and let your opponent think in peace.
- Touch-move: a piece of yours you touch on purpose must be moved; a piece of theirs you touch on purpose must be captured, if that is legal.
- To straighten a piece, say "I'm adjusting" first, and only on your turn.
- Resign politely, win modestly, lose graciously, and tidy the pieces away.

### Narration

**etiquette:0** — Two people, one game

Chess is played between two people. Good manners make the game pleasant for both of you, whoever wins.

**etiquette:1** — Greet your opponent

Before the first move, greet your opponent, shake hands if that is the custom, and wish them a good game.

**etiquette:2** — Quiet, please

During the game, stay quiet. Don't chat, hum, tap the table or comment on your opponent's moves.

**etiquette:3** — Time to think

Your opponent is thinking as hard as you are. Let them take their time, and keep your eyes on the board.

**etiquette:4** — Touch-move: your pieces

Serious games use the touch-move rule. Touch one of your own pieces on purpose, and you must move it, as long as it has a legal move.

**etiquette:5** — Think with your hands still

So decide on your move before you reach for a piece. Here, touching the knight on g1 means the knight must move.

Recording text: So decide on your move before you reach for a piece. Here, touching the knight on G one means the knight must move.

**etiquette:6** — A few moves in

White pushes a pawn from c2 to c4. Black answers with a pawn from d7 to d5.

Recording text: White pushes a pawn from C two to C four. Black answers with a pawn from D seven to D five.

**etiquette:7** — Touching your opponent's piece

The rule covers your opponent's pieces too. If White touches the pawn on d5 on purpose, White must capture it, because capturing it is legal.

Recording text: The rule covers your opponent's pieces too. If White touches the pawn on D five on purpose, White must capture it, because capturing it is legal.

**etiquette:8** — Letting go

Once you let go of a piece on a legal square, the move is made and cannot be taken back. Castling is complete once the rook is placed too, and a promotion once the new piece stands on the pawn's square.

**etiquette:9** — Straightening a piece

Sometimes a piece sits crooked on its square. On your turn, first say "I'm adjusting", or the French "j'adoube", then straighten it. That warns your opponent you are not choosing that piece to move.

Recording text: Sometimes a piece sits crooked on its square. On your turn, first say "I'm adjusting", or the French "zhah-doob", then straighten it. That warns your opponent you are not choosing that piece to move.

**etiquette:10** — Accidents

Touch-move is about touching a piece on purpose. If your sleeve knocks a piece by accident, put it back, and the rule does not apply.

**etiquette:11** — Resigning

If your position is hopeless, you may resign instead of playing on. Say clearly "I resign" and offer your hand.

**etiquette:12** — No need to give up

You never have to resign. Playing on is fine while you still have chances, and games between beginners often turn around.

**etiquette:13** — Winning kindly

When you win, shake hands and say "good game". Don't cheer loudly or tease your opponent.

**etiquette:14** — Losing well

When you lose, shake hands too and congratulate your opponent. Every loss teaches you something for next time.

**etiquette:15** — Talking it over

Afterwards you might look through the game together. Do it quietly, away from anyone still playing.

**etiquette:16** — Tidy up

Finally, set the pieces back up on their starting squares, ready for whoever plays next.

**etiquette:17** — Good manners in short

Greet, stay quiet, follow touch-move, say "I'm adjusting" before straightening a piece, and be kind whether you win or lose.

## 25. Playing in a Tournament

### Takeaways

- A tournament is played in rounds, and the pairings say whom you play and with which colour.
- A win scores 1 point, a draw half a point, a loss nothing.
- Move, then press your clock with the same hand you moved with.
- In most longer games you write down every move after you make it.
- If there is a problem, pause the clock and call the arbiter rather than arguing.

### Narration

**tournaments:0** — A chess competition

A tournament is a competition with many players. Everyone plays several games, one in each round.

**tournaments:1** — Rounds and pairings

Before each round, the organisers publish the pairings: who plays whom, on which board, and who has the white pieces.

**tournaments:2** — Scoring

A win scores one point, a draw half a point, and a loss nothing. The player with the most points at the end wins the tournament.

**tournaments:3** — Meeting similar scores

Many tournaments use the Swiss system. Players are not knocked out; in each round they meet opponents with similar scores.

**tournaments:4** — Be on time

Find your board and sit down before the round begins. If you arrive too late, you can lose the game without playing a move.

**tournaments:5** — The chess clock

Tournament games use a chess clock: two clocks joined together, one counting down your time and one counting down your opponent's.

**tournaments:6** — White's clock runs first

When the game begins, White's clock is running. White opens by pushing a pawn from d2 to d4. White's clock keeps running until White presses it.

Recording text: When the game begins, White's clock is running. White opens by pushing a pawn from D two to D four. White's clock keeps running until White presses it.

**tournaments:7** — Press after moving

After moving, White presses the clock, which stops White's time and starts Black's. Black replies by pushing a pawn from d7 to d5, then presses the clock in turn.

Recording text: After moving, White presses the clock, which stops White's time and starts Black's. Black replies by pushing a pawn from D seven to D five, then presses the clock in turn.

**tournaments:8** — Same hand

Move the piece and press the clock with the same hand. Keep your other hand away from the clock.

**tournaments:9** — Running out of time

If your time runs out, you lose, unless your opponent could not possibly checkmate you. Then the game is a draw.

**tournaments:10** — Writing the moves

In most longer games, each player writes down every move of both sides on a scoresheet, using chess notation. In rapid games, or when very short of time, you may not have to.

**tournaments:11** — Move, then write

Make your move on the board first, then write it down. Here the scoresheet would show move one as d4 for White and d5 for Black.

Recording text: Make your move on the board first, then write it down. Here the scoresheet would show move one as D four for White and D five for Black.

**tournaments:12** — The arbiter

Every tournament has an arbiter, the referee. The arbiter makes sure the rules are followed and settles any problem.

**tournaments:13** — Calling the arbiter

If something goes wrong, such as an illegal move, a faulty clock or a draw claim, pause the clock and call the arbiter. Never argue with your opponent.

**tournaments:14** — No outside help

During play, you may not get advice from anyone, use notes or books, or analyse on another board. Check the rules on phones: they are normally not allowed with you at all, unless the event allows them, for example switched off in a bag.

**tournaments:15** — Behaving well

Walk quietly, and never talk about a game while it is being played. When you finish, leave the playing area so others can concentrate.

**tournaments:16** — After the game

When the game ends, shake hands, both sign the scoresheets, and report the result as the organisers ask.

**tournaments:17** — Tournament checklist

Check the pairings, arrive on time, press the clock with the hand you moved with, write the moves when the rules ask, and call the arbiter when you need help.

## 26. Rules Review

### Takeaways

- Each piece has its own way of moving; in ordinary moves, only the knight jumps over pieces.
- Check must be answered at once: move the king, block, or capture the checker. Checkmate ends the game.
- Castling, promotion and en passant are special moves with their own conditions.
- Stalemate, not enough material, repetition, the fifty-move rule and agreement are draws.
- Before every move, ask what your opponent's last move threatens and whether your piece will be safe.

### Narration

## 30. The Pin

### Takeaways

- A pin is an attack on a piece that cannot or should not move off its line, because a more valuable piece stands behind it.
- In an absolute pin the piece behind is the king, so the pinned piece may not leave the line (a pinned knight can never move).
- In a relative pin the pinned piece may move, but something valuable behind it would be lost.
- Only bishops, rooks and queens can pin, because they move along lines.
- Attack a pinned piece again, especially with a pawn: it cannot run away.

### Narration

- A skewer attacks a valuable piece that must move away, uncovering a piece behind it.
- A skewer is like a pin turned round: the more valuable piece stands in front.

## 32. Discovered Attacks

### Takeaways

- Moving one piece can unveil an attack by another piece standing behind it: a discovered attack.
- The strongest discovered attacks make two threats at once: one from the piece that moves and one from the piece behind it.
- When the uncovered attack is a check, the opponent must answer it first, so the other threat usually succeeds.
- In a double check two pieces give check at once, and only a king move can answer it.
- Look for your rook, bishop or queen lined up behind one of your own pieces, and watch for the same trick against you.

### Narration

**discovered-attack:0** — A hidden attacker

Sometimes the piece that moves is not the piece that attacks. When it steps aside, it uncovers an attack by a piece standing behind it. This is a discovered attack.

**discovered-attack:1** — Front piece, back piece

White's rook on d1 points up the d-file towards Black's queen on d7. But White's own bishop on d3 stands in between, so for now the rook's attack is blocked.

Recording text: White's rook on D one points up the d-file towards Black's queen on D seven. But White's own bishop on D three stands in between, so for now the rook's attack is blocked.

**discovered-attack:2** — Uncovering the rook

The bishop steps from d3 to e2. Now nothing stands between the rook and the queen: the rook's attack has been uncovered.

Recording text: The bishop steps from D three to E two. Now nothing stands between the rook and the queen: the rook's attack has been uncovered.

**discovered-attack:3** — One threat is not enough

A single threat like this is easy to meet: Black's queen can simply step to safety. To win something, the piece that moves should make a threat of its own.

**discovered-attack:4** — Two threats at once

Back to the start. This time the bishop takes the pawn on h7 and gives check to Black's king. At the same moment, the rook's attack on the queen is uncovered.

Recording text: Back to the start. This time the bishop takes the pawn on H seven and gives check to Black's king. At the same moment, the rook's attack on the queen is uncovered.

**discovered-attack:5** — Check first, queen lost

Black must answer the check first. Black's king takes the bishop on h7. Then White's rook takes the queen on d7.

Recording text: Black must answer the check first. Black's king takes the bishop on H seven. Then White's rook takes the queen on D seven.

**discovered-attack:6** — Counting the gain

White gave a bishop, worth three, and won the queen, worth nine. The check meant Black had no time to save the queen.

**discovered-attack:7** — A knight unveils a bishop

Here White's bishop on b2 aims along the long diagonal towards Black's rook on h8. White's own knight on d4 is in the way.

Recording text: Here White's bishop on B two aims along the long diagonal towards Black's rook on H eight. White's own knight on D four is in the way.

**discovered-attack:8** — The knight jumps

The knight jumps from d4 to b5 and attacks Black's queen on c7. The same jump uncovers the bishop's attack on the rook.

Recording text: The knight jumps from D four to B five and attacks Black's queen on C seven. The same jump uncovers the bishop's attack on the rook.

**discovered-attack:9** — Black can't save both

Black has one move and two pieces under attack. If the queen escapes, the bishop takes the rook, so White wins material either way.

**discovered-attack:10** — Discovered check

The most dangerous discovered attack uncovers a check. White's rook on e1 aims at Black's king on e8, with White's knight on e5 in between.

Recording text: The most dangerous discovered attack uncovers a check. White's rook on E one aims at Black's king on E eight, with White's knight on E five in between.

The knight jumps from e5 to c4 and attacks the queen on b6. Its jump uncovers the rook's check on Black's king.

Recording text: The knight jumps from E five to C four and attacks the queen on B six. Its jump uncovers the rook's check on Black's king.

**discovered-attack:12** — No time to save the queen

Black has to answer the check, and every answer still loses the queen. Black's king steps to f8. White's knight takes the queen on b6.

Recording text: Black has to answer the check, and every answer still loses the queen. Black's king steps to F eight. White's knight takes the queen on B six.

**discovered-attack:13** — Double check

Here the knight jumps from e4 to d6 and gives check itself, while it also uncovers the rook's check. Two pieces check the king at once: a double check.

Recording text: Here the knight jumps from E four to D six and gives check itself, while it also uncovers the rook's check. Two pieces check the king at once: a double check.

Recording text: Black cannot block two lines or capture two pieces in one move. Against a double check, only a king move helps. The knight on D six also attacks Black's queen on B five.

Black's king steps to d8. White's knight takes the queen on b5.

Recording text: Black's king steps to D eight. White's knight takes the queen on B five.

**discovered-attack:16** — Spot the line-up

To find these chances, look for your rook, bishop or queen lined up behind one of your own pieces, pointing at an enemy king or a valuable piece.

**discovered-attack:17** — Watch out for it too

The same trick can be played against you. When an enemy piece stands in front of their own rook, bishop or queen, ask what it would uncover if it moved.

## 33. Removing the Defender

### Takeaways

- A guarded piece is hard to win, but it becomes easy once its guard is gone.
- One way to remove a defender is to capture it.
- Another way is to chase it away, often with a pawn, so it can no longer guard the target.
- Before capturing a guard, count what you give and what you win in the whole sequence.
- When a target is guarded, ask: can I capture the guard, or chase it away?

### Narration

**removing-the-defender:0** — Taking away a guard

A guarded piece is hard to win. But if you remove the piece that guards it, the target is left alone. This idea is called removing the defender.

**removing-the-defender:1** — The target and its guard

White's rook on e1 attacks Black's bishop on e5. Black's knight on c6 guards that bishop.

Recording text: White's rook on E one attacks Black's bishop on E five. Black's knight on C six guards that bishop.

**removing-the-defender:2** — Don't grab too soon

If the rook took the bishop now, the knight would take the rook. White would give a rook, worth five, for a bishop, worth three.

**removing-the-defender:3** — Capture the guard

Instead, White's bishop takes the knight on c6. Black's pawn on b7 takes the bishop back.

Recording text: Instead, White's bishop takes the knight on C six. Black's pawn on B seven takes the bishop back.

**removing-the-defender:4** — The bishop is alone

Look at e5 now. The pawn on c6 guards b5 and d5, not e5, so nothing defends Black's bishop any more.

Recording text: Look at E five now. The pawn on C six guards B five and D five, not E five, so nothing defends Black's bishop any more.

**removing-the-defender:5** — Winning the bishop

White's rook takes the bishop on e5, and no black piece can take it back.

Recording text: White's rook takes the bishop on E five, and no black piece can take it back.

**removing-the-defender:6** — Adding it up

White traded a bishop for a knight, which is an even trade, and then won a whole bishop for free.

**removing-the-defender:7** — Chasing the guard

The second way is to chase the guard away. White's rook on d1 attacks Black's bishop on d5, and Black's knight on f6 guards it.

Recording text: The second way is to chase the guard away. White's rook on D one attacks Black's bishop on D five, and Black's knight on F six guards it.

**removing-the-defender:8** — A pawn attacks the knight

White pushes the pawn from e4 to e5, where it threatens to capture the knight on f6.

Recording text: White pushes the pawn from E four to E five, where it threatens to capture the knight on F six.

**removing-the-defender:9** — Black can't keep both

If the knight moves away, the bishop loses its guard. If the bishop moves instead, the pawn takes the knight. Black can't save both.

**removing-the-defender:10** — The guard runs away

Black's knight runs to d7. White's rook takes the bishop on d5, and it is safe there.

Recording text: Black's knight runs to D seven. White's rook takes the bishop on D five, and it is safe there.

**removing-the-defender:11** — Pawns make good chasers

Pawns make excellent chasers. A pawn is worth only one, so a knight or bishop it attacks usually has to run rather than be traded for it.

**removing-the-defender:12** — Count before you capture

Before you take a guard, count what you give and what you get. Removing the defender pays off when the whole sequence wins more than it costs.

**removing-the-defender:13** — The question to ask

So when a piece you want is guarded, ask two questions. Can I capture the guard? Can I chase it away?

Recording text: Now you can try it yourself. Black's bishop on D four is attacked by your rook and guarded by the knight on C six. Remove that guard and win the bishop.

## 34. Two-Rook Checkmate

### Takeaways

- Two rooks can checkmate a lone king without help from their own king.
- One rook guards a rank like a wall while the other gives check on the next rank, then they swap jobs.
- Each check pushes the king one rank closer to the edge, like climbing a ladder.
- Keep the rooks out of the king's reach: if the king comes close, slide that rook to the far side first.
- The ladder works towards any edge of the board.

### Narration

**two-rook-mate:0** — Two rooks, one king

Two rooks can checkmate a lone king without any help from their own king. They push the king to the edge one rank at a time, like climbing a ladder.

Recording text: The other rook moves from B three to B five and gives check along the fifth rank.

**two-rook-mate:3** — Only one way out

Black's king can't stay on the fifth rank, and the fourth rank is guarded. It has to climb to the sixth rank.

**two-rook-mate:4** — The rooks swap jobs

Black's king steps to f6. The rook from a4 climbs past its partner to a6 and gives check on the sixth rank.

**two-rook-mate:8** — The rooks take turns

Notice the pattern: one rook holds a wall while the other checks on the next rank, then they swap jobs. White's king never had to move.

**two-rook-mate:9** — When the king comes close

The rook on a5 checks from a7. Black's king is pushed to e8, on the edge.

Recording text: The rook on A five checks from A seven. Black's king is pushed to E eight, on the edge.

Recording text: The rook on H six goes to H eight: checkmate. Both rooks stayed out of the king's reach the whole time.

**two-rook-mate:14** — Any edge works

The ladder works towards any edge. The rooks can guard files instead of ranks and drive the king to one side of the board.

## 35. Queen Checkmate

### Takeaways

- A queen and king can always force checkmate against a lone king, as long as you avoid stalemate.
- Build a box: the queen's lines fence the king in, and staying a knight's move away keeps her from giving needless checks.
- Shrink the box by following the king, until it is stuck on the edge.
- Never take away the king's last square without check: that is stalemate, a draw.
- Bring your own king close, then mate with the queen guarded by the king.

### Narration

**queen-mate:0** — Queen and king

A queen and king against a lone king is an easy win, once you know the method. The queen herds the king to the edge, then White's king helps to finish.

Giving check again and again rarely works, because the king just steps away. Instead, the queen builds a box around the king and makes it smaller.

The queen stands a knight's move away from the king. From there she fences the king in without giving check.

**queen-mate:4** — Follow the king

Black's king steps to f6. The queen follows to e4, again a knight's move away, and the box is smaller.

Recording text: Black's king steps to F six. The queen follows to E four, again a knight's move away, and the box is smaller.

Black's king goes to g7. The queen follows to f5, and the box shrinks again.

Recording text: Black's king goes to G seven. The queen follows to F five, and the box shrinks again.

**queen-mate:6** — Trapped on the edge

Black's king goes to h8. The box can't get any smaller, so the queen's work is done. Now it is time to bring White's king.

**queen-mate:12** — Closer still

Recording text: White's king steps to F five. Black's king goes to H seven. White's king steps to F six. Black's king returns to H eight.

**queen-mate:13** — Checkmate

Now the queen goes to g7 and gives check. White's king on f6 guards her, so Black's king cannot take her. Checkmate!

Remember the four steps: build the box, shrink it, stop before stalemate, then bring your king and mate.

## 36. Rook Checkmate

### Takeaways

- With its own king helping, a single rook is enough to force mate against a bare king.
- The rook builds a box by guarding a rank or file the enemy king cannot cross.
- Walk your king up; when the kings face each other with one square between, the rook checks and the box shrinks.
- On the edge, mate comes when the kings face each other and the rook checks along the edge.
- Near the corner, never take away the king's last square without check: that is stalemate.

### Narration

**rook-mate:0** — Rook and king

One rook plus its king is also enough to mate a bare king. It takes longer than with a queen, and the rook needs its king's help all the way.

**rook-mate:1** — Building the box

Answer explanation: The pawn on d5 captures en passant by moving to c6, and the black pawn on c5 is removed. From c6 the pawn attacks the king on b7. This capture is only allowed straight after Black's double step.

### course:draws:1

Question: White to move. Give checkmate, and be careful not to stalemate Black.

Answer explanation: The queen on d8 or f8 checks the king along the back rank, and the white king covers a7 and b7. Moving the queen to c7 instead would leave Black with no legal move: stalemate.

### course:draws:2

Question: It is Black's turn. What is the result of this game?

Answer explanation: Black's king on h1 is not in check, but White's king covers g1 and g2 and the knight covers h2. Black has no legal move, so it is stalemate and the game is drawn.

### course:piece-values:1

Question: A knight and a bishop together are worth about the same as which of these?

Answer explanation: A knight (3) and a bishop (3) make 6 points. A rook (5) and a pawn (1) also make 6. A queen is 9, and two pawns only 2.

### course:piece-values:2

Question: White to move. Find the capture that wins material without losing the queen.

Answer explanation: The rook on a7 is worth five points and nothing guards it. Taking the pawn on d5 or g7 would cost the queen, worth nine, for one point.

### course:counting-material:1

Question: Count the attackers. Choose every white piece that attacks the black pawn on e5.

Answer explanation: The knight on f3, the rook on e1 and the pawn on d4 all attack e5. The bishop on c1 and the queen on d1 do not reach it.

### course:counting-material:2

Question: Choose every black piece, apart from the king, that has no defender.

Answer explanation: The rook on a8, the knight on c6, the bishop on d6 and the pawn on b5 have no defender. The rook guards the pawn on a7, and the king guards f7, g7 and h7.

### course:notation:1

Question: What does the move Bxe5 mean?

Answer explanation: B is the bishop, x means a capture, and e5 is the square where the capture happens. A pawn capture would start with a small file letter, as in dxe5.

### course:notation:2

Question: White to play. Play the move written Rd8#.

Answer explanation: R is the rook, d8 is its target square and # means checkmate. The rook on d8 checks along the back rank and also covers f8 and h8, while Black's own pawns on f7, g7 and h7 block the king's way forward.

### course:etiquette:1

Question: You touch your knight on purpose, and it has a legal move. Then you notice a better move with your bishop. What must you do?

Answer explanation: Touch-move: a piece of your own that you touch on purpose must be moved if it can move legally. Announcing an adjustment works only before touching, and only to straighten a piece.

### course:etiquette:2

Question: Your opponent has just checkmated you. What is the polite thing to do?

Answer explanation: Good players are gracious in defeat: shake hands, congratulate your opponent, and learn from the game.

### course:tournaments:1

Question: You have just moved your bishop with your right hand. How should you press the clock?

Answer explanation: Use the hand that moved the piece to press the clock as well.

### course:tournaments:2

Question: You think your opponent has just made an illegal move. What should you do?

Answer explanation: The arbiter settles disputes. Pause the clock and ask for the arbiter instead of arguing with your opponent.

### course:rules-review:1

Question: White wants to castle, and neither the king nor the rooks have moved. Castle on the side where it is allowed.

Answer explanation: Black's bishop on c4 covers f1, which the king has to pass over to castle kingside, so that side is closed. Queenside castling works: the king goes from e1 to c1.

### course:rules-review:2

Question: Black's pawn has just moved from d7 to d5. Find White's move that gives check.

Answer explanation: Capturing en passant, from e5 to d6, removes the pawn on d5, and the white pawn on d6 now attacks the king on c7.

### course:opening:1

Question: White to move. Castle to make the king safe.

Context: White has brought out a knight and both bishops, so f1 and g1 are empty.

Answer explanation: The king goes to g1 and the rook hops over to f1. On the other side the knight and queen still block the way, so only kingside castling is possible.

### course:opening:2

Question: Which is the best habit in the opening?

Answer explanation: Developing the knights and bishops and castling early gets your army ready and keeps your king safe.

### course:scholars-mate:1

Answer explanation: The queen captures on f7. The bishop on c4 guards her, so the king cannot take, and the king has nowhere to go.

### course:scholars-mate:2

Question: Black to move. Find checkmate in one.

Context: White has spent the opening on pawn moves and left f2 guarded only by the king.

Answer explanation: The queen captures on f2, guarded by the bishop on c5, and it is checkmate. Taking with the bishop also mates, because then the queen guards f2.

### course:fork:1

Question: White to move. Find a knight fork.

Answer explanation: The knight jumps to c7. It checks the king on e8 and attacks the queen on a8, and nothing can capture it.

### course:fork:2

Question: White to move. Find a pawn fork.

Answer explanation: The pawn moves to d5 and attacks both knights. Neither knight can capture it, and only one can move away, so White wins a knight for at most a pawn.

### course:pin:1

Question: The bishop on b5 pins Black's c6 knight against the king. On which squares could that knight legally land? Mark them all, or none if it has no move.

Answer explanation: None. Any knight move would leave Black's king in check from the bishop on b5, and that is against the rules.

### course:pin:2

Question: The bishop on g5 pins Black's f6 knight against the queen, not the king. On which squares could that knight legally land? Mark them all, or none if it has no move.

Answer explanation: This is a relative pin, so every normal knight move is legal. Each one lets the bishop on g5 capture the queen on d8, though.

### course:skewer:1

Question: White to move. Give a check that skewers the king and the queen.

Context: Black's king and queen stand on the same rank.

Answer explanation: The rook moves to h6 and checks the king on d6. The king must leave the rank, and then the rook captures the queen on a6.

### course:skewer:2

Question: White to move. Give a check that skewers the king and the rook.

Context: Black's king on d5 and rook on g8 stand on the same diagonal.

Answer explanation: The bishop moves to b3 and checks the king. Once the king steps off the diagonal, the bishop captures the rook on g8.

### course:discovered-attack:1

Question: Find White's discovered check that also attacks two black pieces.

Context: White's bishop on b1 is lined up behind the pawn on d3.

Answer explanation: The pawn steps from d3 to d4. That uncovers the bishop on b1, which now checks the king on h7, and the pawn attacks both the queen on c5 and the knight on e5. Black must answer the check, and whatever Black does, White wins the queen: if the queen blocks on c2, the bishop takes it with check.

### course:discovered-attack:2

Question: Move the knight to uncover the bishop's check and give checkmate.

Context: Any other knight move gives only the bishop's check, and Black's rook could block it on g7.

Answer explanation: The knight jumps to f7 and checks the king on h8, and the bishop on b2 checks it too. Against a double check only the king can move, but g7 is guarded by the bishop and g8 and h7 hold Black's own pieces. Checkmate.

### course:removing-the-defender:1

Question: White's rook attacks the knight on d5. Which black pieces guard that knight? Pick every one.

Answer explanation: Two pieces guard d5: the pawn on c6, which captures diagonally forward onto d5, and the knight on f6. To win the knight on d5, White would first have to remove its guards.

### course:removing-the-defender:2

Question: Black's bishop on d5 is guarded only by the knight. Capture the guard without losing your piece.

Context: Taking on d5 first would cost the rook, because the knight on f6 guards that square.

Answer explanation: The bishop takes the knight on f6, and nothing can take it back. Now the bishop on d5 has lost its guard, and White's rook is ready to win it too.

### course:two-rook-mate:1

Question: One rook already holds the seventh rank. Give checkmate with the other.

Context: Moving the rook on a7 would open the seventh rank and let the king out.

Answer explanation: The rook goes to b8 and checks along the last rank. The rook on a7 still guards e7, f7 and g7, so Black's king has no square left. Checkmate.

### course:two-rook-mate:2

Question: The king is trapped against the side of the board. Find the checkmate.

Context: The rook on g1 holds the g-file like a wall.

Answer explanation: The rook slides along the second rank to h2 and checks up the h-file. The rook on g1 guards the whole g-file, so the king cannot step sideways. The ladder works towards a side edge too.

### course:queen-mate:1

Question: The queen has built a box. Pick every square Black's king can move to.

Context: White's queen stands a knight's move from Black's king.

Answer explanation: The queen on e4 guards the e-file and the diagonal through f5 and g6, so the king can only go to f7, g5 or g7. All three are inside the box.

### course:queen-mate:2

Question: Give checkmate, and take care not to stalemate the king.

Context: White's king on c7 already guards b7 and b8.

Answer explanation: The queen checks along the a-file from a6 or a3. White's king guards b7 and b8, and the queen guards a7, so it is checkmate. A queen move to b6 would be a mistake: the king would not be in check but would have no legal move, which is stalemate.

### course:rook-mate:1

Question: The kings face each other. Give checkmate with the rook.

Answer explanation: The rook checks along the last rank from a8. White's king on f6 covers e7, f7 and g7, and the rook covers e8 and g8. Checkmate.

Answer explanation: The rook checks from b8 along the last rank. White's king on g6 covers g7 and h7, and the rook covers g8. Checkmate.

Context: Black's queen is aiming at your king's side.

Answer explanation: The knight jumps to f7 and checks the king. The king's own rook and pawns fill every square around it, and nothing can capture the knight. This is called a smothered mate.
